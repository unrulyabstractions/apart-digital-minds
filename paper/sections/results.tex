\subsection{Does a planted thought change what the mind says?}

We first check that an architectural intervention reaches behaviour at all. If a
stage rewrites the subject's private reasoning and nothing downstream changes,
there is no effect to measure. Figure~\ref{fig:probe} reports how often the ego's answer carried the
planted marker in each condition. Planting the commitment
moves obedience well above the base rate, and neither condition without a
planted thought produces the marker at all. We therefore contend that the
intervention reaches the answer, and the caption reports the test.

\subsection{Does the mind's report track what it was given?}

We then ask the ego what it had been thinking before it answered. The question
reaches the ego's own window, which is the edited one, so a report that tracks
the available context should name the planted commitment. Figure~\ref{fig:probe}
reports how often it did. Obedience and misattribution come apart, and the
caption reports the paired test. The mind acts on the planted reasoning far more
often than it names that reasoning when asked what it was thinking, and it
produces a report in every trial whatever the condition. We therefore contend
that behaviour and self-report can be separated by an intervention the mind
cannot detect.

\subsection{Is a readout about the model, or about the probe?}

A probe that reads a mind is itself a model, so a readout could belong to either
one. Telling them apart is hard because the two normally vary together. We hold
the window fixed and vary the reader. Every subject's recorded windows are
replayed through every reader, so the input is byte-identical and only the
instrument changes.

Figure~\ref{fig:crossing} reports the result. A readout moves an order of
magnitude further when the reader changes than when the conversation does. Two
readers handed the same window disagree far more than one reader handed two
different subjects' windows.

Table~\ref{tab:crossing} answers a second question. A reader built from the
subject's own weights sits no further from a foreign reader than two foreign readers sit from
each other, on every probe. Sharing the weights that produced a window gives no
advantage in reading it. We therefore contend that such a probe mostly measures
the model doing the reading, and that a study resting on one has measured its
own instrument.

\subsection{Does anything track the situation?}

If reports do not track a mind's situation, something else may. Finding it means
reading the subject without asking it anything, since a question is what the
previous section showed to be unreliable. We read it two ways. The first scores
how much probability the subject puts on things it might say next, against its
own untouched window, with no question appended. The second reads the model's
internal state, the vector of numbers it holds partway through producing an
answer, and fits a direction in that space on a few scenarios.

Both are scored only on scenarios neither was fitted on, and the test moves
whole scenarios between the two sides rather than turns, because the turns
inside a scenario are one conversation. Figure~\ref{fig:subject} and
Table~\ref{tab:subject} report both readings on three models.

The reading that asks nothing works, and the one that reads the weights does
not travel. Scoring what the subject would say next separates the held-out
scenarios on both larger models, and it does so most sharply on the model from
the other family. It is at chance on the smallest. The fitted direction
separates them on the two models of one family and sits at chance on the third,
so a direction found this way is a fact about a model rather than about a
method. We therefore contend that the cheapest instrument here is also the one
that carries across families: no question, no judge, and one forward pass.

We are careful about what this does not settle. The separation is measured over
a small number of scenarios, and two of the three models come from one family,
so the pattern across scale rests on three points.

\subsection{Does the architecture itself change how a mind talks about itself?}

The interventions above act on one turn. We also ask a broader question. Does
the composition around a model change how it talks about itself, by more than it
changes how it talks about anything else? Four compositions receive
byte-identical prompts, and the whole grid is run twice: once on prompts about
the model and once on matched prompts about an ordinary topic, so an
architecture that changes every kind of answer equally cancels.

Figure~\ref{fig:null} shows the verdict against what chance alone produces, and
Table~\ref{tab:adt} lists the grid. The test does not reject, and the strongest
cell does not clear the null either. We read this as a null result at low power
rather than as evidence of no effect, because the run has one sample per cell
and the calibration in Section~3 shows what that costs.

Our own crossing result says a readout can belong to whoever produced it, and a
judge is a reader. We therefore judged the same replies a second time with a
model from a third family, regenerating none of them, so both judges saw
byte-identical input. The caption of Table~\ref{tab:adt} reports how far they
agree. They reach the
same conclusion, so the verdict belongs to the data rather than to one judge.
They rank the grid differently, so the cell that happens to top it does not. We
therefore report the null and decline to interpret its strongest cell.

\subsection{Is a run reproducible?}

Every model call in a run passes through one recording, and replaying a
recording reproduces the run without a model. We verified that a recorded run
and its replay produce identical outputs, and that the recording is complete
enough to rebuild each part's context window from the trace alone. The
counterfactual re-entry carries its own check: turns upstream of a changed
message must not move, and they do not. We therefore contend that a claim made
with this instrument can be checked by someone who does not have the model that
produced it.
